SF Healthcare Career Ladders

Which entry-level healthcare jobs in San Francisco lead to a living wage

What this dashboard answers

A first healthcare job is a real achievement. The question here is what comes next: which entry roles in San Francisco lead to a job that pays a living wage, and which tend to plateau. Choose a household type at the top right: the wage pages (The need, Which jobs clear the bar, Career ladder) recalculate against that living wage. Workforce composition does not depend on it, and the progression metric is defined on the single-adult benchmark.

  1. The need: who works full time and still earns below a living wage.
  2. Which jobs clear the bar: healthcare occupations whose typical entry education is below a bachelor's degree, by SF wage.
  3. Career ladder: which occupations people move to when they leave each entry role, and what those occupations typically pay in SF.
  4. Who holds the jobs: the workforce on each rung, by race and sex.
  5. Measuring progress: a living-wage progression metric for a training program.

Who works on each rung

By race, entry-level healthcare work in San Francisco is done disproportionately by Asian and Black workers. The LVN and RN rung looks much more like the city's workforce as a whole. Both are mostly women, as healthcare is generally.

Measuring progress: the living-wage progression rate

Two measures, reported together because they answer different questions. Coaches can use them to see who is stalled below the line, program leads to compare cohorts, and funders to see an outcome that goes past placement.

Living-wage progression rate (headline)Of participants whose hourly wage at placement was below the benchmark, the share with a verified hourly wage at or above it at the 24-month follow-up. People already above the line at placement are excluded, so they cannot inflate it
Living-wage attainment rateOf all participants placed, the share at or above the benchmark at the 12-month follow-up, and separately at the 24-month follow-up
Follow-up timing12 and 24 months after placement, each within a two-month window either side; the most recent verified wage in the window is used. Each rate is measured at that follow-up, not "at any point by" it
DenominatorsEveryone in scope, including those not reached. Unreached participants are reported as their own category, never dropped
WageHourly wage from the follow-up record; if only annual earnings exist, earnings ÷ (usual weekly hours × weeks worked)
Wage changeMedian change in hourly wage from placement to each follow-up, reported alongside so progress that stops short of the line is visible
BenchmarkMIT SF single adult, fixed at the value published when the participant was placed
Tiers alongsideBelow living wage; single adult; two earners with one child; two earners with two children
DisaggregationRace/ethnicity, sex, entry role, program, reported by default rather than on request. Cells under 10 suppressed and noted, so small groups are protected without dropping out of the count
Data neededPlacement date, occupation (SOC), hourly wage and hours at placement and each follow-up, follow-up status

Why federal indicators are not enough on their own

A WIOA performance score is achieved performance divided by a negotiated target, not an outcome rate. San Francisco's Adult median-earnings target for PY2022 and PY2023 was $7,600 a quarter (EDD Workforce Services Information Notice WSIN22-14). Forty hours a week for 13 weeks at the single-adult living wage comes to about $16,400 in 2024 dollars. A program can beat its federal target and its median participant's quarterly earnings can still sit well below a full-time living-wage quarter. That reflects the measure, not any program. The living-wage progression rate asks the question the federal measures leave open: not did they find work, but did the work lead somewhere.

User manual

Getting around

  • Pages are the tabs under the title. Each page answers one question, listed on the Overview.
  • Living wage for (top right) sets the benchmark for the Overview tiles, The need, Which jobs clear the bar and Career ladder. Who holds the jobs does not depend on a wage, and the metric on Measuring progress is defined on the single-adult benchmark. Values are MIT Living Wage for San Francisco County, hourly, per working adult. "Single adult" is the default and the least demanding household type: a wage that misses it misses every single-earner household.
  • Hover (or tap on a phone) any bar, dot or bubble for its exact values and sample details.
  • Table view under each chart shows the same numbers as a table, for screen readers, copying, or checking.
  • Career ladder: click a bubble or use the role menu to see where people in that role go next.
  • The page remembers your theme choice on this device. Links like #ladder open a page directly.

What each measure means

Median, 25th, 75th percentileEDD OEWS hourly wage percentiles for the SF-San Mateo-Redwood City area. The 25th percentile marks the lower end of an occupation's pay range; OEWS covers all workers, not new hires, so it indicates rather than measures entry pay
OpeningsEDD projected total openings 2023-2033: growth plus people leaving the occupation or the labour force
Projected transfers out per yearA constructed ratio, not an observed rate: EDD's projected occupational transfers 2023-2033 (workers projected to leave for a different occupation) ÷ 10 years ÷ 2023 employment

Caveats to keep in mind

  • National moves, local wages. Transition shares are national and from 2002-2015; wages are San Francisco 2026. They describe how occupations connect, not current SF odds.
  • Only people who changed occupation are in the transition shares. Staying put is not counted.
  • Split occupations are divided evenly. Where one 2010 occupation became several 2018 occupations, moves are split evenly across the successors. The crosswalk does not say how workers actually divided, so this is an assumption; weighting by SF employment instead moves no figure by more than 0.25 points.
  • Resumes over-represent people with degrees, which probably overstates upward moves for every role. Comparisons between roles are more reliable than any single level.
  • Survey uncertainty. ACS figures carry margins of error; unreliable cells are flagged, not hidden.
  • Public data describes the market, not any program's participants. Participant records are what answer where a program's own alumni go.
